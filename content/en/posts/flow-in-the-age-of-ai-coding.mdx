**TL;DR**

> - Flow isn’t tied to manual coding. It comes from deep engagement.
> - AI shifts the challenge away from syntax and toward ambiguity, design, and trade-offs.
> - What matters is uninterrupted ownership of the problem, even when execution is delegated.

Recently I’ve noticed a concern popping up more and more among developers.

“If AI is doing the coding, will we lose the flow?”

The reasoning usually goes like this. Coding is what pulls engineers into a flow state. You struggle with a problem, try different approaches, get stuck, get unstuck, and eventually something clicks. That struggle is the challenge. Remove the coding, and you remove the challenge. No challenge, no flow.

At first glance, that sounds reasonable. But I’m not sure it’s true.

## Coding as a path to flow, not the source of it

For many of us, coding has been the main way to experience flow. You sit down, open your editor, and time starts behaving strangely. Hours pass without you noticing. Your focus narrows. Everything else fades into the background.

That matches what we usually hear about flow. Skills and challenges should be balanced. Too easy is boring. Too hard is stressful. Somewhere in the middle, flow appears.

But when Mihály Csíkszentmihályi wrote about flow, he didn’t reduce it to skills versus challenges in a mechanical way. He collected stories. People reported flow while painting, playing music, climbing, and even while waiting in line at a pharmacy.

Flow was not tied to a specific activity or tool. It was tied to full engagement.

## When the day disappears

Before AI, most of my workdays looked almost identical.

I woke up at 6am. Meditated for an hour. Had breakfast. Read tech news or a book for about 30 minutes. At 8am, I sat down in front of my computer and started working.

Then, somehow, after 5 minutes it was already 4pm.

It always felt like I had just started. In reality, a lot had happened. I had written code, made big and small commits, joined meetings with the CEO and the architect, talked with the development team, reviewed pull requests, had lunch, and pushed things forward.

The "5 minutes" feeling is obviously an exaggeration, but the distortion of time is real. That is one of the symptoms of being in flow.

## AI changed my workflow, not that feeling

Today, my workflow is heavily shaped by AI tools.

I write more specifications and less code.

I review more pull requests than I author.

I spend more time thinking about system design, boundaries, and trade-offs.

Still, the feeling is the same.

My working hours feel short.

I get a lot done.

At the end of the day, I’m often surprised that it’s already evening.

The tools changed. The experience didn’t.

## How attention moves instead of disappearing

One thing I’ve noticed is that my attention doesn’t always stay in the same place anymore.

Sometimes I closely follow how an AI agent thinks and codes. I read through its steps, not just the result. I do this either to learn from the approach or to validate that the reasoning makes sense.

Other times, the opposite happens. While one agent is working on a task, I’m already preparing the next specification. This feels extremely fast and efficient, but I mostly do this when I clearly understand what the agent is doing and how it is doing it.

What matters here is not speed, but trust and clarity.

When I trust the process, my attention can move forward without breaking focus. When I don’t, I slow down and stay close to the execution. In both cases, I’m still engaged. I’m still thinking. The flow doesn’t stop. It just shifts.

This made me realize that flow is less about constant hands-on activity and more about uninterrupted ownership of the problem.

## Flow does not belong to coding

This is the part I find most important.

If flow depended on manual coding, I should feel less engaged now. Less challenged. Less satisfied with my work. That hasn’t happened.

The challenge is still there. It just moved. Instead of fighting syntax, I’m fighting ambiguity. Instead of solving local problems, I’m shaping systems. Instead of writing lines of code, I’m deciding what should exist at all.

I’ve written before about how I structure my workdays to protect deep focus, using the Flowmodoro approach. What surprised me is that those same principles still apply when the work shifts from coding to higher-level thinking.

Flow seems to care much less about *what* tool you use and much more about whether your attention is fully occupied by meaningful work.

## A different kind of craftsmanship

For some developers, less hands-on coding may genuinely reduce enjoyment. That is a valid experience. But it’s not the only possible outcome.

For others, flow simply moves upward. From implementation to intent. From code to systems. From execution to direction.

At least, that’s been my experience so far.

I’m curious how others see it.

Does AI change your flow, or does it just change where it shows up?
