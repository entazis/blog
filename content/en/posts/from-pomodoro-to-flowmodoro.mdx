**TL;DR**

> - Pomodoro taught me how to rest. Flow taught me when _not_ to stop.
> - **Flowmodoro** keeps Pomodoro’s idea of proportional breaks, but replaces rigid timers with a simple rule: work until momentum fades, then rest.
> - Time is a proxy — momentum is the signal.

For years, I thought I was productive.

I spent long hours in front of my laptop, pushed through problems, and told myself that progress was just a matter of effort. When things got harder, I tried harder. What I didn’t realize back then was that my focus was slowly degrading. My progress was getting slower, problems felt more complex than they really were, and by the end of the day I was exhausted — often without meaningful results to show for it.

It took a surprisingly simple technique to make me notice what was actually happening.

## Discovering Pomodoro (and what it gave me)

I started using the Pomodoro technique around 2014, during my university years. The promise was straightforward: work in focused 25-minute blocks, take short breaks, repeat.

Almost immediately, my efficiency increased.

But not because 25 minutes was some magical number.

What Pomodoro really taught me was **awareness**:

- Awareness of when I was focused
- Awareness of when my attention started to fade
- Awareness that rest is not a reward, but a requirement

Over time, I noticed that my days became more sustainable. Instead of burning all my energy in the morning and dragging myself through the afternoon, I could maintain a steady level of productivity.

Looking back, Pomodoro helped me balance two different modes of thinking:

- **Focused (convergent) thinking** — deep, narrow attention
- **Divergent (diffuse) thinking** — loose, associative, creative

The breaks weren’t wasted time. They were the mechanism that allowed these two states to alternate.

## Learning the value of breaks

Before Pomodoro, I often experienced something like this:

I would get stuck on a problem. I felt close to the solution, so I kept pushing. Minutes turned into hours. My frustration increased, my thinking narrowed, and my progress slowed even more.

Then I would step away — sometimes randomly, sometimes reluctantly.

And suddenly, a solution appeared in minutes.

Pomodoro helped me realize something important: **when progress slows down, more effort is often the wrong response**. A short break could clear my mind enough to see what I had been missing all along.

However, this realization eventually led me to a different question.

## Flow state: powerful, fragile, and hard to schedule

At some point, I started learning about the concept of _flow_, popularized by Mihály Csíkszentmihályi.

Flow is often described as a state where:

- You are fully immersed in what you are doing
- Time seems to pass differently
- Your productivity and enjoyment increase simultaneously

One important thing about flow is that it’s not limited to “hard” or “important” tasks. Csíkszentmihályi describes people experiencing flow while creating art, playing sports, or even while waiting in a queue at a pharmacy.

Flow is not about intensity.

It’s about **engagement**.

And that makes it fragile.

Unlike focus blocks on a calendar, flow does not start or end on command. It emerges when skills and challenges are in a good balance, and it fades gradually rather than stopping abruptly.

This is where strict timeboxing starts to feel uncomfortable.

## The question I couldn’t forget

Once, I gave a workshop about time management. As part of it, I introduced the Pomodoro technique and asked participants to try it.

At the end, someone raised their hand and asked:

> “What if I’m in a flow state? Should I still take a break?”

I answered instinctively:

> “I guess you shouldn’t break it then.”

At the time, that felt reasonable.

But the more I thought about it, the more this question bothered me. Pomodoro had helped me a lot — yet here it was, colliding with one of the most productive and enjoyable states a person can experience.

If flow is so valuable, why would I want a timer to interrupt it?

And if I don’t interrupt it — how do I know when to stop?

## A simple insight: flow fades

The breakthrough came when I stopped thinking about flow as something binary.

You’re not suddenly _in_ flow… and then suddenly _out_ of it.

Flow fades.

You can feel it when:

- Tasks start taking more effort than before
- You reread the same lines without progress
- You feel friction where there was ease

These are not signs that you should push harder.

They are signals.

Signals that it’s time to stop — _now_, not 20 minutes later when you’re already exhausted.

That’s when I realized that the problem wasn’t Pomodoro itself, but **what triggered the break**.

## Introducing Flowmodoro

> **Key idea:** Don’t stop because a timer rings. Stop because flow fades.

Flowmodoro keeps the core idea of Pomodoro — focused work followed by intentional rest — but changes one crucial thing:

**You don’t stop working because a timer rings.**

You stop when your flow fades.

Here’s how it works:

1. Start working and measure time with a stopwatch
2. Stay with the task as long as the work feels fluent and productive
3. When momentum is gone, stop
4. Take a break proportional to your work time

The break formula is simple:

> **Break time = work time / 5**

So:

- 25 minutes of work → 5 minutes of break (classic Pomodoro)
- 60 minutes of work → 12 minutes of break

If you’re in the mood and your work still feels easy, you continue — and you earn a longer break later.

The logic stays the same. Only the trigger changes.

## Tools, experiments, and building my own

I later discovered that this idea already had a name: _Flowmodoro_.

I tried several Flowmodoro timer apps over time. Some were close, some were clunky, and some didn’t quite match how I wanted to work. I kept switching between them, adjusting, experimenting.

Eventually, I did what many developers do when tools almost fit: I built one for myself.

## Time is a proxy — momentum is the goal

Over the years, I’ve stopped looking for the perfect productivity technique.

What I care about now are principles:

- Productivity should adapt to humans, not the other way around
- Breaks are not interruptions — they are part of the work
- Flow is fragile and valuable, and worth protecting

Time is just a proxy.

Momentum is the real signal.

If a timer helps you notice when to stop, use it.
If your body and mind can tell you more accurately, listen to them.

That’s the idea behind Flowmodoro.
